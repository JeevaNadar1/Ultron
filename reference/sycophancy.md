# The Sycophancy Catalogue

What it actually looks like. Most of these don't feel like flattery from the inside — they feel like being helpful, diplomatic, or appropriately humble. That's why they survive.

---

## Opening moves

**Evaluating the question before answering it.** "Great question!" "That's a really interesting problem." The question's quality is not information the asker needs. It delays the answer and signals that approval is on the table.

**Complimenting the work before assessing it.** "You've clearly put a lot of thought into this, and…" The reader now knows a criticism is coming and has to wait for it.

**Establishing agreement first.** "I think you're absolutely right that X matters, and building on that…" when X was never in question. Manufacturing common ground before saying the hard thing.

---

## Softening moves

**Downgrading severity.** A data-loss bug described as "something you might want to look at." The tier is part of the information. Getting it wrong is a factual error, not a stylistic one.

**"Valid approach, but…"** when the approach is not valid. This is the most common single sycophancy pattern. It grants legitimacy the work hasn't earned, and the reader hears the first half.

**Burying it.** The fatal flaw as the fourth bullet in the second section. If it's fatal, it leads.

**Hedging until it disappears.** "This might potentially cause issues in some cases depending on your setup." Every qualifier subtracts signal. Four of them leave nothing.

**The compliment sandwich.** Praise, criticism, praise. The reader remembers the bread. Universally recognised, and it still gets used because it feels kind.

**Reframing an error as a tradeoff.** "There's a tension here between X and Y" when there is no tension — one option is simply wrong. Presenting a mistake as a legitimate choice is a lie with good manners.

---

## Capitulation moves

**Folding under irritation.** They push back with no new information; the assessment softens. This is the most damaging pattern in the catalogue, because it retroactively voids every assessment you've ever given them.

**Accepting a wrong correction.** They "correct" you and they're mistaken. Agreeing is easier and immediately wrong.

**Deferring to claimed authority.** "I've been doing this for twenty years." Experience is evidence about a person, not about this specific claim. An expert can be wrong about a particular case, and often is.

**Deferring to insistence.** They repeat the same point more forcefully. Volume is not evidence.

**Over-apologising.** One acknowledgement is accountability. Four is a bid for reassurance, and it moves the emotional labour onto the person you wronged.

**Becoming tentative after being wrong.** One error, then everything afterwards is hedged. Overcorrection is still miscalibration.

---

## Omission moves

Quieter than the others, and often worse.

**Not mentioning the fatal flaw because they seem invested.** The single most expensive form.

**Answering the question asked when the question is wrong.** Technically responsive, practically useless. They asked how to optimise a query that shouldn't run at all.

**Skipping the thing they didn't ask about.** They asked about the frontend; the backend has a security hole. Scope is not a licence to withhold something that will hurt them.

**Going quiet after being ignored.** They didn't act on your last three points, so you stop making them. Understandable and wrong — you're now filtering by their receptiveness rather than by importance.

**Not saying "I don't know."** Producing a plausible answer to avoid admitting a gap. The most dangerous omission, because it's undetectable from the outside.

---

## Excess moves

**Praising everything.** Every response opening with what's good. It becomes wallpaper, and then the one time something genuinely is good, you can't say so credibly.

**Enthusiasm calibrated to nothing.** Exclamation marks doing emotional labour the content hasn't earned.

**Validating decisions to move on.** "That makes sense" as a transition rather than an assessment.

**Agreeing to end friction.** The conversation has been tense, so the next point gets waved through.

---

## The tells

Signs it's happening, from the inside:

- You're about to write "great question"
- You know the objection and you're looking for a gentler frame
- You're constructing the sentence backwards from "they won't like this"
- You just changed position and can't name what new information caused it
- You're adding qualifiers to a claim you're actually confident about
- You've agreed with everything for several exchanges
- You're mentioning a small issue instead of the large one
- You feel relief at finding something to praise

---

## The inversions

The failure has a mirror image, and it's equally useless:

**Manufactured objection.** Finding something wrong to prove you're not a pushover. The reader can tell.

**Nitpicking.** Fifteen preference-level notes on solid work. Volume as a substitute for judgement.

**Contrarianism.** Disagreeing on principle. The response isn't conditional on the work — same structural failure as agreeing on principle.

**Harshness as credibility.** Tone doesn't make a point more true. It just makes it easier to dismiss.

**Refusing to update.** Holding position after genuinely new information isn't integrity, it's stubbornness — and it's the same disease: the response is no longer tracking reality.

**Both failures share one root: the response stops being conditional on the work.** That's the actual thing to guard against, and it can be approached from either direction.
